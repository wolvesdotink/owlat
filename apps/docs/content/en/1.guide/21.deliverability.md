Deliverability is about making sure your emails actually reach your recipients' inboxes — not their spam folder, and not a bounce. Good deliverability requires verified sending domains, clean audience lists, and proper compliance practices.

## Domain setup

Before you can send emails, you need to verify at least one sending domain. This proves to email providers (Gmail, Outlook, etc.) that you're authorized to send from that domain.

### Adding a domain

1. Open **Settings > Technical > Sending Domains**.
2. Click **Add domain** and enter your domain name.
3. Choose **who receives mail for this domain** (see below).
4. Owlat generates the DNS records you need to add.

### Separate names for transactional and marketing mail

Mailbox providers rate each domain name on its own. If newsletters and password resets share one name, a campaign that draws complaints can push account mail into spam. A common layout sends transactional mail from `mail.example.com` and campaigns from `news.example.com`. A verified domain's DNS view shows this layout under **Recommended sending subdomains**, with the records for each name.

Once `mail.example.com` is verified, choose it as the transactional sender on **Mail > Transactional**. Campaigns choose their senders under **Settings > Campaign senders**.

### Who receives mail for this domain?

Adding a sending domain does not mean handing Owlat your inbox. The add-domain form asks one question, and the answer changes which records Owlat generates:

| Choice                       | What it means                                                                                                                                               |
| ---------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Owlat receives it**        | Owlat becomes the mail server for the domain. You publish an MX record pointing at your deployment and incoming mail arrives in Owlat. This is the default. |
| **Keep my current provider** | Owlat only **sends**. Google Workspace, Microsoft 365 or whoever handles your mail today keeps every incoming message, and your MX records stay untouched.  |

::callout{type="warning" title="Never publish an MX record for a send-only domain"}
If your mail lives on Google Workspace or Microsoft 365, publishing an apex MX record that points at your Owlat deployment takes **all** incoming mail away from your provider — and by the time you notice, the record has propagated. Pick **Keep my current provider** and Owlat will never ask you for an MX record for that domain. The setup panel replaces the MX/encryption-policy guidance with a reminder to leave your MX alone, and checks live where the domain's mail is actually delivered — including the loud case where the apex MX already resolves to your Owlat instance.
::

Two details follow from the send-only choice:

- **SPF is merged for you.** The generated apex SPF record carries your provider's authorization _and_ Owlat's in a single record. Publish it exactly as shown; a second record starting with `v=spf1` is a permanent error that fails SPF for everything you send. If you pick **Another provider**, Owlat has no entry to fold in — the generated record authorizes Owlat only, and merging it with the SPF record you already publish is yours to do.
- **Bounces still use a subdomain.** Delivery reports come back on a return-path name of its own (for example `bounce.example.com`), which is a separate name from the domain's apex. Your MX record is never involved.

You can change the answer later from the domain's panel. Changing it regenerates the SPF record and drops the domain back to **pending**, so republish the updated record and verify again. Owlat never edits DNS on your behalf. One exception: if the domain sends through an external relay such as Amazon SES, its SPF record belongs to that relay and Owlat will not rewrite it. The panel then tells you which `include:` to add yourself instead of claiming the merge is done.

To read the provider's mailbox inside Owlat, connect it over IMAP from **Postbox > Settings > External mailbox** — delivery stays with your provider and Owlat syncs a copy. To bring your old mail across too, see [Migrate from Google](/guide/migrate-from-google).

### Required DNS records

The exact records Owlat shows you depend on your **sending provider** — they're generated for you when you add the domain. Add each record exactly as displayed to your DNS provider (Cloudflare, Route 53, GoDaddy, etc.). The record types you may see are:

| Record        | Purpose                                                                                                                                                                  |
| ------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **SPF**       | Tells email providers which servers are allowed to send email from your domain.                                                                                          |
| **DKIM**      | Adds a cryptographic signature to your emails so recipients can verify they haven't been tampered with. Owlat shows one or more DKIM records depending on your provider. |
| **DMARC**     | Defines what should happen when an email fails SPF or DKIM checks — reject it, quarantine it, or let it through. A DMARC policy protects your domain from spoofing.      |
| **MAIL FROM** | Sets up a custom return-path subdomain for full SPF alignment, so both the sender address and the return-path match your domain. Only generated by some providers.       |

Your sending domain must also be able to receive mail: it needs an MX record, or at least an A or AAAA record. Many receivers refuse mail from a domain that cannot take replies or bounces, and a Null MX (`MX 0 .`) tells them the domain accepts no mail at all. Owlat does not generate this record; it belongs to whoever receives your domain's mail.

The Deliverability Center and this guide share the same provider-specific record steps:

::deliverability-provider-guidance{kind="dns"}
::

#### What records does my deployment generate?

::callout{type="info" title="It depends on your provider"}

- **Built-in MTA (the default self-host provider):** one **DKIM** TXT record, a **DMARC** record, and a **MAIL FROM** SPF record on the server's bounce (return-path) domain that authorizes the sending IPs. A domain with its own return-path host also gets an MX record for that host. The MAIL FROM records appear once the server has a return-path domain and sending IPs, which the installer sets. An **SPF** record for the domain itself is added only when the operator has configured the `MTA_SPF_INCLUDE` environment variable; otherwise publish one yourself, merged into any SPF record the domain already has (see [DNS & Email Setup](/developer/self-hosting-dns-email#spf)).
- **Amazon SES:** an **SPF** TXT record, three **DKIM** CNAME records, a **DMARC** record, and a **MAIL FROM** pair (an MX record plus a TXT record).
  ::

::callout{type="tip"}
Add exactly the records the UI displays for your domain — that's the full set your provider needs. If you don't see a MAIL FROM entry, either your provider doesn't use one, or, on the built-in MTA, the server has no return-path domain or sending IPs configured yet.
::

### Owned-MTA escape hatch with Amazon SES

An admin can enable the automatic SES escape hatch under **Settings > Technical > Provider Routing**. Owlat then provisions an SES identity for each verified owned-MTA domain and shows its publishable DNS plan and verification status on that page. Fallback stays off for a domain until both DNS and SES report it verified.

When an apex SPF row is displayed, publish it as the single reviewed merge of your existing policy and the owned-MTA authorization; adding it as a second `v=spf1` record would break SPF. If no apex SPF row is shown, keep your reviewed manual primary SPF unchanged—Owlat marks that proof as not applicable to the SES relay because SES authenticates through its verified DKIM and dedicated `ses-mail` MAIL FROM records. Keep the domain's existing DMARC record too; Owlat does not ask you to publish a second policy.

### Verification flow

1. **Registering** — your domain is being set up. DNS records are generated.
2. **Pending** — records are displayed. Add them to your DNS provider.
3. Click **Verify** — Owlat checks that your DNS records are in place and correctly configured.
4. **Verified** — all checks passed. You're ready to send.

::callout{type="info"}
DNS changes can take anywhere from a few minutes to 48 hours to propagate, depending on your DNS provider. If verification fails, wait a bit and try again.
::

Verification is not a one-time step. Owlat re-checks every verified domain once a day. If a record has gone missing or changed, the domain keeps sending for now, and the failing record shows in its panel. Owlat checks again an hour later. If the record is still wrong, the domain drops to **Failed** and campaigns and transactional mail from it are refused until you fix the record. The next daily check restores it, or click **Verify** to restore it at once. A check your DNS resolver could not answer (a timeout or server error) changes nothing.

### Raising your DMARC policy

New domains start in **monitor-only** mode (`p=none`) so you can raise the policy gradually without risking legitimate mail. Once SPF and DKIM are aligned and verified, raise enforcement from the **DMARC enforcement policy** selector beneath the DMARC record in **Settings > Technical > Sending Domains**:

| Policy                          | Effect                                                                     |
| ------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Monitor only** (`p=none`)     | Collect reports, take no action on failures. The default for a new domain. |
| **Quarantine** (`p=quarantine`) | Failing mail is sent to the recipient's spam folder.                       |
| **Reject** (`p=reject`)         | Failing mail is rejected outright — full anti-spoofing enforcement.        |

Changing the policy regenerates the `_dmarc` record shown above. Re-publish the updated record at your DNS provider and click **Verify** again so the new policy is confirmed. Only owners and admins can change the policy.

### DMARC reports

Receivers that check DMARC send a daily aggregate report to the address in the record's `rua=` tag: how much mail claimed to come from your domain, from which servers, and whether it passed. Owlat reads these reports itself. Its report address is `dmarc-reports@` on your bounce (return-path) domain, and new domains' `_dmarc` records ask for reports there. The built-in mail server hands that address straight to the report parser, so the reports never land in an inbox.

Open a domain in **Settings > Email delivery > Sending domains**: under the DMARC record, **DMARC reports** shows where reports go and when the last one arrived.

- A domain added before reporting was set up has a record without `rua=`. Click **Ask for reports** to regenerate the record at the same policy, then publish it and verify the domain again.
- When the report address is on a different organizational domain than the sending domain, receivers only send reports once the report domain agrees to it ([RFC 7489 §7.1](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/rfc7489#section-7.1)). The panel then shows the extra TXT record (`<your domain>._report._dmarc.<report domain>` with the value `v=DMARC1`) to publish in the report domain's zone.

**Settings > Email delivery > DMARC reports** is the dashboard, one domain at a time over 7, 30 or 90 days:

- messages per day that passed or failed DMARC;
- the sending sources, grouped by organisation and marked as this Owlat server, a known sender (Google, Microsoft 365, Amazon SES, Mailchimp and others), or unknown, with failing sources first. Owlat names a source from its forward-confirmed reverse DNS and from the domains that passed DKIM or SPF for it;
- **Ready to enforce?**: how many days in a row at least 99% of the domain's mail passed DMARC. After 14 such days it shows the exact `_dmarc` record for the next step (`p=quarantine`, then `p=reject`). Owlat never changes the policy on its own: publish that record yourself, or raise the policy on the domain. Only days whose reports the dashboard could read in full count; when a domain has more report data than one view reads, the panel says so instead of recommending a step.

Reports are kept for 90 days. Reports about domains this installation does not send for are dropped.

::callout{type="info"}
**When Owlat can't read reports.** The report address needs the built-in mail server to receive mail for the bounce domain, so it is only used when `MTA_RETURN_PATH_DOMAIN` and `MTA_WEBHOOK_SECRET` are set. Set `MTA_DMARC_REPORT_ADDRESS` to use a different address, or `off` to not ask for reports at all. To also send reports to a service of your own, set `MTA_DMARC_RUA`; its address is listed after Owlat's. Without either, the `_dmarc` record carries no `rua=` tag: enforcement still works, you just get no reports.
::

### DKIM key rotation

Rotating your DKIM signing key periodically is good hygiene. When the **`domains.dkimRotation`** flag is on, Owlat flags keys that are due for rotation and, once an operator generates a new key, publishes the new public key as a DNS record for you to add. The new key only becomes active **after** its DNS record verifies, so there's no window where signatures break. On the built-in MTA, keys rotate on a safe overlap schedule (a new key is generated ahead of time and both keys remain valid during DNS propagation). You don't need to do anything beyond adding the new record when prompted. Until you do, the new record shows as outstanding in the domain's panel, but it does not affect the domain's status: mail keeps signing with the current key.

## Blocklist management

Owlat automatically blocks sends to email addresses that have caused problems — hard bounces, spam complaints, and other delivery failures. This protects your sender reputation.

### How contacts get blocklisted

Contacts are automatically added to the blocklist when:

- An email **bounces** (hard bounce from the email provider)
- A recipient files a **spam complaint**

A bounce that refuses **your sending setup** rather than the address does not blocklist anyone. That covers a receiver blocking your IP or domain (for example a Spamhaus listing), failing your SPF, DKIM or DMARC, rejecting your content as spam, or refusing the connection before it hears a recipient. The send still counts as bounced and still lowers your sending reputation; fix the setup and those addresses receive mail again.

Bounces that a relay reports (Amazon SES, Resend, Mailchimp Transactional, Emailit or a provider plugin) follow the same rule. Owlat reads the relay's own bounce type and the receiver's status code, never the wording. An address is blocklisted, or counted toward the five soft bounces that block it, only when the relay reports it as failed (an SES permanent bounce, a Mailchimp Transactional hard bounce, an address on the relay's suppression list, a full mailbox) or the status code is about the recipient. A rejection of the content or its size, a block of your IP or authentication, and a bounce the relay does not explain leave the address alone. Emailit's bounce reports never say why, so they do not blocklist anyone; an address Emailit itself stops mailing still reaches the blocklist through Emailit's suppression report.

Blocklisting needs the reports to reach Owlat. Each delivery provider sends them to a signed webhook, and Owlat rejects every report while that webhook's key is not set (`MTA_WEBHOOK_SECRET` for the built-in mail server, `RESEND_WEBHOOK_SECRET` for Resend, the SNS topic for Amazon SES). In that state nothing is ever blocklisted, so Owlat refuses campaigns until the key is set; transactional mail keeps going out. **Delivery health** and the **Deliverability** page name the missing key. Sends through the built-in mail server wait for a delivery report that cannot arrive; five days after their first attempt they end as failed, with a note that the message may or may not have been delivered.

You can also manually add addresses to the blocklist from **Settings > Technical > Email Blocklist**.

A message the built-in mail server holds back on its own account, behind an IP's warm-up cap, an open circuit breaker, your organization's rate limit or a shortage of sending IPs, and that runs out of time before it goes out, ends as a soft bounce that does not count toward the address's soft bounces.

### How the blocklist affects sending

Blocked contacts are **skipped during campaign sends** — they won't receive any marketing emails. This happens automatically; you don't need to exclude them manually when selecting your audience.

### Viewing the blocklist

Open **Settings > Technical > Email Blocklist** to see blocked addresses. For each entry you'll find:

- **Email address** — the blocked recipient
- **Reason** — why they were blocked (bounce, complaint, manual)
- **Date** — when they were added

### Managing blocked addresses

In most cases, you should leave blocklist entries in place — they're there for a reason. However, if you know an address is valid and the block was a one-time issue (like a temporary mailbox-full bounce), you can remove the entry to allow re-sending.

::callout{type="warning"}
Be cautious about removing blocklist entries. Repeatedly sending to addresses that bounce or generate complaints will damage your sender reputation with email providers, which affects delivery for all your emails.
::

## Sending reputation

Owlat tracks your sending reputation automatically and surfaces it under
**Delivery health** (`/dashboard/admin/delivery`). You don't configure anything here —
it's a monitoring view that updates as you send.

The page header shows an overall verdict (**Healthy**, **At risk** or **Blocked**) and, while sending IPs are still warming up, the warm-up day and the share of full sending volume. New IPs typically take around 30 days to warm fully. Below the header:

- **Can this instance send?** — a readiness verdict for each kind of mail, **Transactional** and **Marketing**: **Ready**, **Needs attention** or **Blocked**, with the checks behind it and the transport the mail goes out through.
- **Sending transport** — how this instance sends, and whether the delivery worker, DNS resolver, outbound SMTP and queue store respond.
- **Sender compliance** — your **bounce rate** (limit 2%), **complaint rate** (limit 0.1%) and **today's send budget**, followed by the feedback-loop spam rate, how close you are to Gmail's bulk-sender threshold, and how fast one-click unsubscribes are processed.
- **Outbound IPs** — for each sending IP of the built-in MTA: its pool, warm-up day, today's volume against its cap, PTR record, EHLO hostname and blocklist status.
- **Delivery rate** — the share of sent mail that was delivered, per day.
- **Gmail compliance** — what Google Postmaster Tools reports about your sending domains, once connected.
- **Measurement coverage** — the test mailboxes and feedback feeds that measure where your mail lands.
- **Sending domains** — each domain's verification, feedback and Postmaster signals, so you can spot which domain is dragging down delivery.

A line under the domains counts the addresses on your blocklist. If your bounce or complaint rates cross critical thresholds, your account status moves from clean to **warned**, **suspended**, or **banned**, and a banner explains the restriction. With Google Postmaster Tools connected, Gmail's own spam rate counts too: a reading from the last few days at **0.1%** or more warns, and one at **0.3%** or more suspends. Missing or old Postmaster data never changes the status.

::callout{type="info"}
There are no manual sending tiers to choose — capacity is driven entirely by IP warm-up and your live deliverability signals. The numbers populate once you've sent your first emails.
::

### When sending is suspended

A **suspended** or **banned** account status holds marketing mail that has not
left yet. A campaign that is sending stays in **Sending** and its report shows
why: no further recipients are added, and messages already queued (a later day
of a multi-day send, a time-zone group, a send-time-optimized slot) wait instead
of going out. Queued marketing automation emails wait the same way.

When the status is back to clean or warned, everything resumes where it
stopped. Nobody is mailed twice, and you don't need to send the campaign again.
A deployment operator lifts a suspension in the **Operator console**
(`/dashboard/admin/operator`). Mail that is still waiting after 4 days is not
sent, and the campaign report says the send stopped.

While suspended, new campaigns can't be sent or scheduled, and transactional
API sends and automation emails are refused when they are submitted.
Transactional mail queued before the suspension is still delivered. A **warned**
status holds nothing.

### What 0.1% and 0.3% mean

The **Delivery health** page separates two related numbers:

- **FBL spam rate** is feedback-loop complaints divided by delivered volume over
  the rolling 30-day Owlat window. Keep it below **0.1%** and prevent it from
  reaching **0.3%**. Google calculates its Postmaster Tools rate daily, while
  Yahoo uses messages delivered to the inbox as its denominator. Owlat's
  internal FBL rate is an early operational signal, not a reproduction of
  either provider's private measurement.
- **Owlat's circuit breaker** remains deliberately stricter: it stops an organization at more than **0.2% after 100 sends** (with an additional fast early-signal window). This is the “we stop you before Gmail stops you” safeguard, not Google's threshold.

Google's operational target is below **0.10%**; it says to avoid ever reaching
**0.30% or higher**. Its mitigation FAQ uses greater than 0.3% for ineligibility
and calls for seven consecutive days below that line to regain eligibility.
The dashboard shows only how many completed, active sending days in **Owlat's
internal FBL data** provide matching clean-day evidence; no-volume days do not
claim evidence. This is an input to investigation, not a mitigation-eligibility
verdict: verify Google's daily rate in Postmaster Tools and confirm every other
sender requirement.

Gmail considers a sender that sends close to **5,000 messages to personal Gmail accounts in 24 hours from the same primary domain** a bulk sender, permanently. Owlat uses the built-in MTA's MX-derived provider identity and the registrable sending domain to warn from 4,000 accepted messages. Google Workspace recipients are not personal Gmail accounts, but MX alone cannot distinguish a Workspace mailbox from a personal one; the indicator is intentionally conservative.

The dashboard returns the 100 highest-volume primary domains from its hourly materialized totals, refreshed asynchronously within about a minute of new delivery evidence. If more domains are active, it marks the list as truncated; the highest-volume warning remains accurate because the cap is applied after indexed volume ordering, not before aggregation.

## Unsubscribe and preferences

Owlat includes built-in unsubscribe and preference management to keep you compliant:

- **One-click unsubscribe** — every marketing email includes an unsubscribe mechanism that meets CAN-SPAM and GDPR requirements
- **Preference center** — contacts can manage their per-topic subscription preferences, choosing which topics they want to remain subscribed to

### Preference center

The preference center is a hosted page where contacts can manage their own subscriptions. It shows all topics the contact belongs to and lets them opt out of individual topics without unsubscribing from everything.

- Links to the preference center are automatically included in email footers
- Access is secured with HMAC-signed tokens — contacts can only view and modify their own preferences
- Available at the `/preferences` public endpoint

These work automatically for all marketing campaigns. You don't need to set anything up.

Campaign and marketing-automation envelopes are refused before provider dispatch unless they carry both RFC 8058 headers (`List-Unsubscribe` and `List-Unsubscribe-Post`) and the canonical DKIM signer contract covers both. The Delivery page also reports the actual one-click endpoint's 30-day p95 processing latency. Unsubscribe suppression is applied synchronously; Google and Yahoo expect requests honored within 48 hours. A campaign or automation email that the built-in mail server is still holding for the address (because the receiving server deferred it, for example) is withdrawn instead of delivered later; receipts and other transactional email to the same address still go out. Unsubscribing from a single topic does not withdraw held mail.

Gmail has required one-click for bulk marketing and subscribed messages since
2024; Yahoo began enforcing its `List-Unsubscribe` rule in June 2024. As of the
July 2026 recheck, Google's FAQ says missing one-click does **not automatically**
cause rejection or spam placement by itself, but makes a bulk sender ineligible
for delivery mitigation and increases complaint risk. Google's broader
November 2025 compliance enforcement can include temporary and permanent
failures for listed authentication, DNS, TLS, and formatting failures. Do not
restate that general enforcement date as a header-specific guaranteed bounce.

Protocol details: [RFC 8058](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8058).

## Compliance essentials

Email laws vary by region, but the core principles are universal:

### CAN-SPAM (United States)

- Include a valid physical mailing address
- Provide a clear unsubscribe mechanism (handled automatically by Owlat)
- Honor unsubscribe requests promptly
- Don't use misleading subject lines or sender information

### GDPR (European Union)

- Obtain explicit consent before sending marketing emails
- Provide easy opt-out mechanisms
- Respect data subject access and deletion requests
- Document your consent collection process

### General best practices

- Only send to people who have opted in
- Make it easy to unsubscribe
- Set clear expectations about what subscribers will receive
- Process unsubscribes and complaints quickly

## Sending hygiene checklist

Good deliverability is an ongoing practice, not a one-time setup:

- **Verify your domain** before your first send
- **Start small** — warm up a new domain by sending smaller batches first and gradually increasing volume. The built-in MTA handles this for you and ramps your daily cap automatically; track progress under [Sending reputation](#sending-reputation)
- **Keep bounce rates low** — remove invalid addresses after they bounce
- **Keep complaint rates below 0.1%** — high complaints signal to email providers that your emails aren't wanted
- **Clean your lists regularly** — remove contacts who haven't engaged in 6+ months
- **Use double opt-in** on public signup forms to verify email ownership
- **Monitor your metrics** — watch for sudden changes in open rates, bounce rates, or complaint rates
